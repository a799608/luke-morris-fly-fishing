About

The guide behind the guide.

Luke Morris is a 28-year-old fly fishing guide based in Aspen, Colorado. He came up on Pennsylvania trout water, graduated from Penn State, traded a corporate desk for a drift boat, and hasn't looked back.

A Pennsylvania kid first.

Luke grew up in the Pocono Mountains — Jim Thorpe, Pennsylvania — where he taught himself to fish at age eight. By his early teens he was fly fishing, mostly self-taught, on the Lehigh River, Mud Run, and the cold tributaries that feed them. He's the kind of angler who learned by being on the water more than anyone else in his age bracket. Not in a fly shop. On the water.

Where he learned.

At Penn State, Luke majored in statistics with a minor in economics, held season tickets for Beaver Stadium, and joined an academic fraternity with friends now scattered across New York, New Jersey, Connecticut, and Philadelphia finance. He also did what every serious angler in State College does: he fished the Central Pennsylvania limestone country every chance he got.

That water is some of the most respected trout habitat in the eastern United States. Spring Creek, the limestone spring stream that runs through State College itself. Penns Creek, the longest limestone stream in Pennsylvania, sometimes called the "Bug Factory" for the density of its hatches. Spruce Creek, where two presidents have fished. Fishing Creek. The Little Juniata. Wild brown trout water, mile after mile of it.

It is also the home water of Joe Humphreys — widely regarded as the Godfather of American fly fishing. Humphreys, born in 1929 in Curwensville, PA, directed Penn State's renowned angling program for nineteen years and is a member of the Fly Fishing Hall of Fame. At 96 years old he is still teaching, still chasing hatches on the same streams. Especially at night, his signature discipline — fishing big browns after dark with mouse and streamer patterns.

Luke didn't study under Humphreys personally. But he came up in the region Humphreys built — fishing the same streams, in the same culture, taking cues from the same tradition. That is the school Luke came out of. Not many Colorado guides can say that.

Catskills, too.

The Beaverkill. The Willowemoc. The upper Delaware system. The literal birthplace of American fly fishing. Luke fished it on multiple occasions during his college and post-college years — chasing trout where Theodore Gordon, Lee Wulff, and the rest of the canon worked out what we now call American fly fishing.

Giving back.

While at Penn State, Luke volunteered with a program teaching disabled veterans with PTSD how to fly fish. It is one of the experiences he points to when asked why guiding feels like a calling and not a job. The water doesn't fix anything by itself. But it gives someone a place to put their attention. That matters.

Then west.

Two years ago Luke left a corporate job in Pennsylvania and moved to Aspen to guide full-time. He now spends his seasons on the Roaring Fork, the Fryingpan, the Colorado, and other Rocky Mountain water in the valley. The fish are different. The water is different. But the craft — the patience, the reading of the seam, the right fly at the right depth at the right minute — is the same craft he learned in Central PA.

If you grew up back east, came up through the Penn State alumni network, or just want a guide who knows what real pizza tastes like and uses the phrase fuhgeddaboudit with sincerity — you'll know within five minutes you've booked the right person.
